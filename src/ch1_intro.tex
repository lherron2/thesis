\section{Biomolecular structure as a problem of distributions}
\label{ch1:sec:biomolecular-distributions}

Historically, structural biology focused on the native structure---the dominant, stable fold of a biomolecule. At the dawn of the field, this description was apt. The early structures of DNA, myoglobin, and tRNA were unambiguously determined as well-folded structures by X-ray diffraction \citep{Watson1953DNA,Kendrew1958Myoglobin,Kim1974tRNA}. As the number of solved molecular structures grew, determining the native structure became the central goal of experimental structure determination and, later, of computational prediction.

Concurrently, theorists sought first-principles descriptions of biomolecular folding. For RNA, dynamic-programming approaches and nearest-neighbor energetic rules showed that secondary structure could be predicted from an explicit decomposition of interaction energies \citep{Nussinov1980FastAlgorithm,Mathews1999ThermodynamicParameters}. More generally, energy-landscape and folding-funnel pictures described folding as motion on a thermodynamic surface whose minima correspond to stable structures \citep{Bryngelson1987ProteinFolding,Wales2018EnergyLandscapes}. Spin-glass-inspired analyses further shed light on how heterogeneous interactions generate ordered, disordered, and metastable phases \citep{Bryngelson1987ProteinFolding,Pagnani2000Glassy}. The common insight is that many biomolecules have multiple accessible configurations, each with an associated thermodynamic weight, rather than a single, dominant fold.

As the field matured, structural heterogeneity was confirmed experimentally: methods such as NMR spectroscopy, single-molecule FRET, and cryo-electron microscopy revealed structural variation that is not explained by a single static model of the structure \citep{Purcell1946NMR,Roy2008SingleMoleculeFRET,Kuhlbrandt2014ResolutionRevolution}. The goals of molecular modeling expanded beyond identifying one dominant structure, shifting towards characterization of the structure ensemble.

The natural description to follow is the equilibrium probability distribution: the function that assigns each microstate, or configuration, a probability at thermal equilibrium. For a 3D molecular structure, a microstate is a set of atomic coordinates; for a spin model, it is a configuration of spin variables. In either case, the equilibrium, or Boltzmann, distribution takes the form \citep{Callen1985Thermodynamics,McQuarrie1976StatisticalMechanics}

\begin{equation}
  \label{thesis_intro:boltzmann}
  p_{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x};\beta)=\frac{e^{-\beta U(\mathbf{x})}}{Z(\beta)},
  \qquad
  Z(\beta)=\int e^{-\beta U(\mathbf{x})}\,d\mathbf{x}.
\end{equation}

The Boltzmann distribution in Eq. \ref{thesis_intro:boltzmann} models how the probability of a system's microstates $\mathbf{x}$ depends on their energy, $U(\mathbf{x})$, and a thermally fluctuating environment captured by the inverse-temperature $\beta$. At low temperatures, low-energy configurations dominate, while at higher temperatures, the distribution flattens, making high-energy states accessible. The conceptual picture is intuitive, but estimating $p_\mathrm{eq}$ numerically proves to be difficult in all but the most trivial cases. With an accessible energy function and temperature in hand, it seems that $p_\mathrm{eq}$ is nearly known. However, the normalization constant $Z$, called the partition function, requires evaluating an integral over the usually high-dimensional configuration space that $\mathbf{x}$ spans.

Fortunately, $Z(\beta)$ is a scalar-to-scalar function unlike $p_{eq}(\mathbf{x};\beta)$. Unfortunately, estimating $Z(\beta)$ still requires sampling $p_{\mathrm{eq}}(x;\beta)$ exhaustively in all relevant regions of configuration space. But, if one has access to $Z(\beta)$, then the entire thermodynamics of the system may be derived: thermodynamic observables are encoded in derivatives of $Z(\beta)$.

Levinthal's paradox illustrates the severity of the sampling problem: a polypeptide 100 residues in length has $10^{47}$ configurations, assuming each residue has 3 conformational states \citep{levinthal1969fold}. The astronomical configuration space makes enumeration or brute-force exploration impossible. Analytical methods from statistical mechanics like the replica trick are useful, but they typically require assumptions that are violated by real biomolecular systems \citep{Parisi1997Replica}. Today's commonly used biomolecular energy functions like AMBER and CHARMM must be complicated in order to account for bonded interactions, electrostatics, van der Waals interactions, and solvent effects \citep{Wang2004GAFF,Vanommeslaeghe2010CGenFF}.

Another complication arises when one does not work in the full atomistic description. In practice, biomolecular structure is often represented in terms of reduced variables or collective coordinates---for example, selected dihedral angles, radii of gyration, or contact distances. These reduced descriptions still follow equilibrium probability distributions, but the effective energy function that generates them is often not straightforwardly available. In that setting, one must model the distribution from samples alone, without requiring access to the energy function that generated them.

Probabilistic generative models provide this framework, offering a way to learn complex equilibrium distributions directly from data or an energy function \citep{Nareklishvili2025GenerativeReview}. The framework supports likelihood evaluation, which makes the partition function accessible and thereby enables thermodynamic reweighting. The next section introduces this class of models and explains the relevance to modeling biomolecular ensembles.

\section{Generative models as a framework for molecular ensembles}
\label{ch1:sec:generative-models}

The previous section identifies the equilibrium distribution (or equivalently the partition function) as the essential object to consider if biomolecular structure is to be described in a way that remains faithful to statistical physics. The difficulty is that such distributions are high-dimensional, the energy landscapes are rugged, and typically accessible only through sparsely available samples. This problem has attracted sustained interest in machine learning, where probabilistic generative models were developed to model complicated probability distributions directly from data.

The setup of probabilistic generative models (PGMs) is as follows. Assume access to configurations drawn from an intractable target distribution, these may be data generated by experiment or simulation. A PGM learns a transformation between the samples from the target distribution and a simple prior distribution, usually Gaussian. Different generative architectures parameterize this transformation in various ways, but the goal is the same: to generate samples from the intractable target via sampling the simple prior, and evaluate the probability of data under the target distribution.

Normalizing flows were among the earliest successful realizations of this idea \citep{Dinh2017RealNVP}. They build the generative map as a composition of invertible transformations, so that the likelihood of a sample can be computed exactly through the change-of-variables formula. Exact evaluation of the likelihood is important because it permits training by likelihood maximization. However, normalizing flows are limited in their expressivity, since the invertibility and likelihood calculation are made possible by restricting the mathematical operations the network performs. Moreover, it was discovered empirically that normalizing flows struggle to transform between a multimodal target distribution and a unimodal prior.

A major advance was to move the invertibility condition from the network architecture to an ordinary differential equation. In continuous normalizing flows, a neural network learns a velocity field that transports particles from Gaussian-distributed initial positions to the target distribution at a final time \citep{Grathwohl2018FFJORD}. Importantly, the likelihood remains accessible by integrating the divergence of that velocity field along the generative trajectory. This continuous-time formulation is more flexible than discrete flows, but still struggles to generate multimodal distributions.

Diffusion models address this difficulty by making the generative process explicitly stochastic. Inspired by non-equilibrium thermodynamics, they define a forward process that progressively destroys structure by adding noise to data, and a reverse process that reconstructs data by removing noise \citep{SohlDickstein2015NonEq,Ho2020DDPM,Song2021ScoreBased}. Because the forward process is stochastic, the learned reverse process can reconstruct disconnected metastable states without the mode-separation problems that limit deterministic models. The broader continuous-time literature now connects diffusion models, flow-matching variants, and related methods through a shared ODE/SDE/PDE perspective on probabilistic transport.

These developments directly impact structural biology. AlphaFold 3, for example, uses a diffusion model as a part in its larger AI model. The success of AlphaFold 3 and contemporary models revealed another limitation. In many problems in molecular science, the main bottleneck is no longer the expressive power of the architecture, but the amount and quality of the available data. Generative models do not extrapolate reliably to entirely new classes or modes; they are only as good as the data on which they are trained. Protein structure prediction benefited from an unusually rich data regime. RNA structure prediction, for example, has 100-fold less data to train on. If the field is now to do more with a relatively fixed amount of high-quality molecular data, structures that promote generalization have to be built into AI and deep-learning models.

\section{The role of physical structure in generative modeling for molecular science}
\label{ch1:sec:physical-structure}

Inductive biases are structural assumptions built into a model that reduce its solution space and, by extension, the amount of data required for learning \citep{Battaglia2018Relational}. In molecular machine learning these biases take several forms. Some are architectural: translation, rotation, and Euclidean equivariances can be built directly into the network \citep{Fuchs2020SE3}. Others enter through the objective function, as the addition of physics-informed loss terms that penalize violations of known constraints \citep{Cuomo2022PINNs}. Probabilistic generative models offer a third route: physical structure can be placed directly into the prior distribution or into the dynamical process that transports between the prior and target.

Boltzmann generators provided an early example of this strategy \citep{Noe2019Boltzmann}. They learn a map between a simple reference distribution and a molecular Boltzmann distribution while retaining enough structure for reweighting and free-energy estimation. In one of the provided applications, the authors scaled the variance of the prior distribution depending on the temperature at which the training sample was obtained. This enabled the model to generate data at multiple temperatures and re-weight samples across temperatures. However, Boltzmann generators require that the energy function be readily available, which limits their application to the full atomic configuration space. If one is to instead use reduced descriptions, a generative model that learns directly from temperature-dependent data, without requiring the energy function, is required.

One of the central contributions of this thesis is the Thermodynamic Maps framework (Chapter~4), which trains a temperature-dependent diffusion model directly on data. The key idea is to encode temperature as an inductive bias built directly into the generative prior as a temperature-dependent Gaussian. Each training sample is paired with the temperature at which it was collected, and the prior variance is adjusted accordingly during training. Once trained, a Thermodynamic Map trained on samples from only a small number of temperatures can then be evaluated at intermediate or extrapolated temperatures by modifying the prior variance. Chapter~4 demonstrates this on the two-dimensional Ising model, where a Thermodynamic Map trained on data from only two temperatures--one above and one below the critical point--recovers the temperature dependence of magnetization and heat capacity across the full range, including the phase transition. The same framework is then applied to RNA conformational sampling, where the object of interest is not a single structure but a temperature-dependent conformational ensemble.

The core idea behind Thermodynamic Maps is easily extended to the other thermodynamic variables, as discussed in the Outlook (Chapter~7). Pressure, chemical potential, and composition can also be represented through physically structured priors as additional terms in the exponent of Eq. \ref{thesis_intro:boltzmann} that adjust the mean and variance of the prior.


Beyond modifying the prior, a second strategy for incorporating physics is to build hybrid modeling pipelines that combine learned and physics-based components. Learned models are efficient proposal mechanisms: they can generate candidate structures, conformations, or poses at low computational cost provided there is sufficient training data. Physics-based models penalize steric clashes, unrealistic geometries, and provide interpretable scores in the form of energy values.

This thesis develops the hybrid physics-AI modeling strategy in two applications. In RNAnneal (Chapter~5), molecular simulations provide the conformational data used to train Thermodynamic Maps, and the learned model supplies a thermodynamic score for ranking candidate RNA structures by consistency with equilibrium. The method is \textit{ab initio} at the level of training data: experimental structures are not used to parameterize the score model. In DiffDock-Glide (Chapter~6), a diffusion model is used to generate candidate protein-ligand poses, and a physics-based docking program refines and rescores them. Although docking is not an ensemble problem in the same sense as biomolecular folding, it still faces a large search space and must filter proposed solutions for physical plausibility.

The thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~2 benchmarks representative probabilistic generative frameworks on molecular systems with tunable dimensionality and multimodality, identifying the regimes in which different architectures succeed or fail. Chapter~3 applies diffusion models to multi-temperature replica-exchange simulations and shows that a diffusion model is capable of extrapolating thermodynamics across temperatures. Chapter~4 develops Thermodynamic Maps as a principled framework for encoding thermodynamic structure into score-based generative modeling and validates the method on both the Ising model and RNA conformational sampling. Chapter~5 presents RNAnneal, which integrates Thermodynamic Maps into a complete pipeline for \textit{ab initio} RNA ensemble prediction and yields interpretable ensemble-level predictions. Chapter~6 extends the same learned-plus-physics philosophy to molecular docking with DiffDock-Glide, especially in challenging out-of-distribution settings. A concluding chapter summarizes the main contributions and outlines directions for future work.
