\section{Biomolecular structure as a problem of distributions}
\label{ch1:sec:biomolecular-distributions}

\lh{lukas}

Structural biology has undergone a gradual but consequential shift in how biomolecular structure is characterized. For much of the field's history, the goal of structure determination was to resolve a single, well-defined native conformation --- the structure deposited in the Protein Data Bank, the fold predicted by a computational model, the geometry refined against crystallographic data. This view served protein science well for decades and remains appropriate for many globular proteins, whose folding landscapes are funnel-like and dominated by a single thermodynamic basin.



But the native-structure-centric view is increasingly understood to be incomplete. Experimental advances in NMR relaxation dispersion, cryo-electron microscopy, and single-molecule techniques have revealed that many biomolecules do not occupy a single conformation under physiological conditions. Instead, they populate ensembles of interconverting structures whose relative weights are determined by the free energy landscape. The appropriate description is not a single set of atomic coordinates but a Boltzmann-weighted distribution over conformational space.

This distinction is not merely semantic. It has practical consequences for how structure informs function. Allosteric regulation, ligand recognition, and catalytic activity can all depend on the population of transiently occupied conformations that are invisible to methods optimized for resolving a dominant state. A complete structural picture must therefore include not only the identities of accessible conformations but also their relative thermodynamic weights --- and how those weights change in response to temperature, pressure, ionic conditions, and ligand binding.

The theoretical basis for the ensemble view draws on the statistical mechanics of disordered systems. A useful framework is the concept of frustration: when competing energetic and entropic contributions to the free energy are of similar magnitude, the resulting landscape is rugged, with many local minima of comparable depth rather than a single dominant funnel. The degree of frustration determines whether a system folds into one conformation or populates many. For proteins, it has been argued that evolution has minimized frustration, producing landscapes with a dominant native basin and a separation of folding timescales that allows fast, reliable folding. Theoretical and computational analyses of RNA folding have reached a different conclusion. RNA energy landscapes can be highly frustrated, with little separation of timescales during folding and no single structure dominating the landscape. The mathematical connection is precise: the Hamiltonians governing RNA base pairing and stacking bear a structural resemblance to spin-glass models, where heterogeneous interactions among components give rise to multiple ground states and complex phase behavior. This analogy is not merely cosmetic --- it has been developed formally in the literature and will provide part of the motivation for the choice of model systems studied in this thesis.

RNA is therefore an especially important case for the ensemble perspective. Short noncoding RNAs such as riboswitches can exist in multiple distinct conformations, none of which need dominate the others, and the conformational equilibrium shifts in response to metabolite binding, temperature, and ion concentration. Furthermore, the structural heterogeneity of RNA presents a direct challenge to high-resolution experimental methods. X-ray crystallography, NMR, and cryo-EM all operate by detecting an average signal from the conformational ensemble. When the ensemble is homogeneous, the average is informative; when it is heterogeneous, the detector receives a superposition of signals that can be difficult to deconvolve. The result is that RNA structures are both harder to determine experimentally and more in need of computational ensemble methods than their protein counterparts. Approximately one hundred RNA-only structures are deposited in the PDB annually, compared to ten thousand for proteins.

The challenge for computational methods, then, is not simply to predict a single fold but to generate a diverse set of possible conformations together with a thermodynamic ranking that accounts for both energetic and entropic contributions to each conformer's free energy. The ranking must be physically grounded: it should reflect the Boltzmann distribution and respond correctly to changes in external conditions. Methods that produce a single predicted structure, however accurate, do not meet this standard. Methods that produce multiple conformations without meaningful weights are only marginally better. What is needed is a computational framework that treats the prediction of molecular structure as what it fundamentally is: a problem of learning a probability distribution.

This thesis develops such a framework. The central methodological insight is that probabilistic generative models --- machine learning models designed to learn and sample from complex distributions --- are a natural tool for this problem, particularly when they are constructed to respect the thermodynamic structure of the distributions they aim to learn. The following section introduces generative models from this perspective, and the remainder of the thesis demonstrates the idea through a series of progressively more challenging applications in the molecular sciences.

\section{Generative models as a framework for molecular ensembles}
\label{ch1:sec:generative-models}

The previous section argued that predicting biomolecular structure is fundamentally a problem of learning probability distributions. This reframing naturally suggests a connection to generative modeling, a branch of machine learning concerned with exactly this task: given samples from an unknown distribution, learn to generate new samples that obey the same statistical structure.

Generative models can be contrasted with discriminative models, which dominate much of applied machine learning. A discriminative model learns a mapping from input to output --- given a protein sequence, predict its fold; given a molecule, predict its binding affinity. These models are powerful but inherently point-predictive: they return a single answer for each query. A generative model, by contrast, learns the distribution itself. Once trained, it can produce diverse samples from the learned distribution, estimate the relative likelihood of different configurations, and in some cases evaluate how the distribution changes under perturbation. For problems where the answer is not a single structure but a weighted ensemble of structures, the generative formulation is the more natural one.

The class of generative models most relevant to this thesis is probabilistic generative models, which learn distributions through invertible transformations. The core idea is to define a mapping between a simple reference distribution --- typically a Gaussian --- and the complex target distribution that one wishes to sample. If the mapping is invertible and its Jacobian is tractable, then samples from the reference can be transported into samples from the target, and the likelihood of any configuration can be computed from the change of measure induced by the transformation. Different generative architectures differ in how they parameterize this mapping: normalizing flows use compositions of structured invertible layers, continuous normalizing flows parameterize the transformation as the solution of an ordinary differential equation, and diffusion models construct it through a stochastic denoising process.

Diffusion models have become the dominant generative architecture across many domains, and they are the primary generative framework used in this thesis. The intuition behind them is appealingly simple. In the forward direction, a diffusion process progressively adds noise to data until the original structure is destroyed and all that remains is a sample from the reference distribution. In the reverse direction, a neural network learns to undo the noise --- to denoise --- at each step, gradually reconstructing structured samples from pure noise. The network does not learn the distribution explicitly; instead, it learns the score, the gradient of the log-probability density, which provides a local direction toward higher-probability regions at each noise level. Once the score is learned, the reverse process can be simulated to generate new samples.

What makes diffusion models particularly interesting for molecular science is a structural analogy to statistical mechanics. The forward diffusion process transports a complex, multimodal distribution to a simple Gaussian, destroying correlations and structure along the way. The reverse process reconstructs that structure from the Gaussian reference. This is reminiscent of what statistical mechanics does conceptually: relating the complex, correlated behavior of a physical system to a simple reference through the partition function and free energy. The Gaussian reference distribution of a diffusion model can be interpreted as the equilibrium distribution of a harmonic oscillator --- a system simple enough to be solved analytically, yet rich enough to carry a temperature dependence. This observation, which will be formalized in Chapter 4, opens the door to building thermodynamic structure directly into the generative model rather than treating it as an afterthought.

There is a second reason diffusion models are well suited to molecular problems: their capacity for multimodal distributions. Molecular systems frequently occupy multiple metastable states separated by kinetic barriers --- a protein with two functional conformations, an RNA with several base-pairing topologies, a ligand that binds in different poses. Generating the full distribution requires a model that can represent and transition between these modes. Normalizing flows, which parameterize continuously invertible transformations, are topologically constrained in their ability to create or destroy modes during the mapping. Diffusion models, because they operate through a stochastic process, do not share this constraint. The noise injection in the forward process erases the topological structure of the data, and the learned reverse process can reconstruct multiple disconnected modes from a single unimodal reference. This flexibility comes at a cost --- diffusion models require many sequential denoising steps to generate a sample, making them slower than single-pass architectures --- but for scientific applications where accuracy matters more than throughput, the tradeoff is favorable.

However, generative models are not interchangeable. Different architectures exhibit different strengths and failure modes depending on the dimensionality of the data, the number and arrangement of modes, the size of the training set, and the complexity of the probability landscape. These differences are well documented in the image and language modeling literatures, but they have received less systematic attention in the molecular sciences, where the distributions of interest have distinctive properties: they live on compact or periodic domains, they obey known physical symmetries, and the data is often severely limited. Understanding which generative framework is best suited to which molecular modeling task is a prerequisite for building on any of them. Chapter 2 addresses this question directly, benchmarking three representative probabilistic generative frameworks --- Neural Spline Flows, Conditional Flow Matching, and Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models --- on molecular systems with tunable complexity. The results of that comparison inform the methodological choices made throughout the rest of the thesis.

\section{The role of physical structure in generative modeling for molecular science}
\label{ch1:sec:physical-structure}

Generative models learn from data. When data is abundant and representative, a sufficiently expressive model can learn the target distribution without any knowledge of the system that produced it. This is approximately the situation in natural image synthesis, where training sets contain millions or billions of examples spanning the space of visual configurations. Molecular science operates in a fundamentally different regime. Experimental structures are scarce --- particularly for RNA --- and computational sampling is expensive. The training data available to a generative model of molecular structure is typically sparse, unevenly distributed across conformational space, and potentially unrepresentative of the equilibrium distribution. A model trained naively on such data will interpolate where the data is dense, fail where it is sparse, and have no mechanism for distinguishing physically meaningful configurations from artifacts of incomplete sampling.

The central argument of this thesis is that this limitation can be substantially overcome by building physical knowledge into the structure of the generative model itself. The idea is not to constrain the model to obey physics as a hard rule --- an approach that would sacrifice the flexibility that makes generative models useful --- but rather to provide the model with inductive biases that encode the known thermodynamic structure of the distributions it is learning. A generative model whose architecture reflects the Boltzmann distribution, whose reference distribution carries a temperature dependence, or whose outputs are refined by a physics-based energy function will generalize more reliably from sparse molecular data than one that treats the same data as structureless vectors. This principle --- that generative models in molecular science are most effective when grounded in physics --- recurs throughout the thesis and motivates both its methodological development and its choice of applications.

The thesis develops this idea through a sequence of studies that progress from methodological foundations to increasingly challenging biological applications. Each study is adapted from a published or submitted manuscript, and each chapter is therefore largely self-contained: some background material, particularly concerning diffusion models and statistical mechanics, reappears across chapters. This introduction provides the shared conceptual argument that links them, and the individual chapters should be understood as independent demonstrations of a common theme rather than sequential sections of a single narrative.

Chapter 2 establishes the methodological starting point by systematically comparing three probabilistic generative frameworks --- Neural Spline Flows, Conditional Flow Matching, and Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models --- on molecular systems of tunable complexity. The study uses a Gaussian mixture model and molecular dynamics trajectories of a short peptide as benchmark systems and evaluates how each framework handles increasing dimensionality, multimodality, and data scarcity. The results show that no single framework dominates across all conditions: Neural Spline Flows are superior at estimating probability density differences but degrade at high dimensionality; Conditional Flow Matching scales best with dimension but struggles with complex multimodal landscapes; and Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models most accurately capture the multimodal torsion angle distribution of the peptide system. This last finding --- that diffusion-based methods are particularly suited to the multimodal, moderate-dimensional distributions typical of molecular systems --- motivates their central role in the chapters that follow.

Chapter 3 makes the first move from benchmarking to application. Having identified diffusion models as especially capable on molecular distributions, this chapter applies a Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model to the problem of learning equilibrium distributions across temperatures from replica exchange molecular dynamics data. The key insight is that temperature can be treated not as a fixed external parameter but as a random variable in the joint distribution of coordinates and inverse temperature. A diffusion model trained on the joint distribution can then generate samples at any temperature --- including temperatures not present in the training data --- by conditioning the generative process on the desired inverse temperature. The chapter demonstrates this on two molecular systems: the Aib9 peptide and GACC tetranucleotide. The model successfully populates metastable states that are absent from the training data at the target temperature and produces free energy differences in good agreement with long unbiased reference simulations. This result demonstrates that a diffusion model can learn meaningful physics from multi-temperature sampling data, but the approach is ad hoc: temperature enters as an additional coordinate rather than through a principled thermodynamic construction.

Chapter 4 addresses this limitation by developing Thermodynamic Maps, the central methodological contribution of the thesis. A Thermodynamic Map is a score-based diffusion model in which the reference distribution is not a fixed Gaussian but a temperature-dependent harmonic oscillator. This construction has a physical interpretation: the forward diffusion process maps the complex target system onto a simple reference system whose temperature dependence is analytically known, and the reverse process reconstructs the complex system from the simple one at any desired temperature. The temperature dependence is not learned from data alone --- it is encoded in the structure of the model through the scaling of the prior distribution. The result is a generative model that respects the thermodynamic identity of the distributions it transports.

The chapter demonstrates the capacity of Thermodynamic Maps to generalize to unobserved physics through two applications. In the first, a Thermodynamic Map trained on Monte Carlo samples of the two-dimensional Ising model at just two temperatures --- one above and one below the critical temperature --- successfully predicts the temperature-dependent magnetization and heat capacity across the full temperature range, including the critical point and its associated divergences. The inferred critical exponents are consistent with the exact Onsager solution. This result is striking because the model has never seen data near the phase transition; it infers the transition purely from the thermodynamic structure encoded in its architecture. In the second application, the chapter introduces Thermodynamic Map-accelerated Molecular Dynamics, an active learning protocol in which a Thermodynamic Map iteratively steers molecular dynamics simulations toward equilibrium. Applied to two RNA systems --- the GCAA tetraloop and HIV-TAR hairpin --- the method recovers conformational landscapes and melting curves using a fraction of the computational resources required by conventional replica exchange simulations. This application represents the first use of Thermodynamic Maps for biomolecular structure and provides the methodological foundation for the work that follows.

Chapter 5 is the culmination of the thesis. It introduces RNAnneal, an \textit{ab initio} framework for predicting three-dimensional RNA structural ensembles from sequence alone. RNAnneal integrates several of the ideas developed in earlier chapters --- diffusion-based generative modeling, thermodynamic scoring, and physics-grounded ensemble ranking --- into a hierarchical pipeline for conformational sampling and evaluation. The method begins by generating a large pool of candidate secondary structures, assembling them into three-dimensional models, and refining them through implicit-solvent molecular dynamics. An ensemble of Thermodynamic Maps is then trained on geometric features of the candidate structures to produce a score that distinguishes experimentally resolved conformations from decoys. The approach is entirely unsupervised: no experimental structures are used during training, and the scoring function is derived from \textit{ab initio} physical models. Evaluated on a benchmark of riboswitches, RNAnneal recovers near-experimental conformations for the majority of systems and provides interpretable measures of ensemble heterogeneity through per-nucleotide interaction entropies. The method also enables de novo prediction of conformational landscapes for systems whose structures have not been experimentally resolved. RNAnneal demonstrates that the combination of generative modeling with physical inductive biases --- the central theme of this thesis --- can address one of the most challenging open problems in computational structural biology.

Chapter 6 broadens the scope of the thesis by applying the same underlying philosophy to a different scientific problem: molecular docking. In structure-based drug design, the goal is to predict how a small molecule binds to a protein target, a problem that involves both generating plausible binding poses and scoring them for physical realism. The chapter develops DiffDock-Glide, a hybrid approach that uses a diffusion-based generative model to sample candidate poses and a physics-based energy function to refine and rescore them. The study demonstrates that this combination substantially improves out-of-distribution performance compared to either approach alone --- the generative model provides broad conformational coverage, while the physics-based refinement corrects steric clashes and restores energetically important interactions that the learned model fails to capture. Although the scientific context is different from the RNA ensemble work in Chapters 3, 4, and 5, the methodological principle is the same: generative models in molecular science benefit from explicit physical structure, and hybrid learned-plus-physics workflows can outperform either component in isolation.

\section{Outline of the thesis}
\label{ch1:sec:outline}

The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows. Chapter 2 benchmarks representative generative frameworks on molecular distributions, establishing which architectures are best suited to the multimodal, moderate-dimensional landscapes characteristic of molecular systems. The chapter provides the empirical basis for the methodological choices that follow.

Having established the comparative behavior of these frameworks, Chapter 3 focuses on Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models as a particularly capable architecture for learning complex molecular distributions. The chapter applies a DDPM to replica exchange molecular dynamics data and demonstrates that the model can generate equilibrium samples at temperatures not present in the training set --- the first indication that diffusion models can learn transferable thermodynamic information from molecular simulations.

Chapter 4 then formalizes this temperature-mixing idea into the Thermodynamic Maps framework, in which the temperature dependence of the target distribution is encoded in the structure of the generative model rather than learned implicitly from data. The framework is validated on the Ising model phase transition and applied to RNA conformational sampling through an active learning protocol, Thermodynamic Map-accelerated Molecular Dynamics.

Chapter 5 builds directly on this framework to address the central biological problem of the thesis: \textit{ab initio} prediction of RNA structural ensembles. The chapter introduces RNAnneal, which combines hierarchical structure sampling, Thermodynamic Map-based scoring, and physics-grounded ensemble ranking to predict three-dimensional RNA conformational ensembles from sequence alone.

Chapter 6, placed as a complementary application, shows that the broader philosophy of combining generative models with physical inductive biases extends beyond thermodynamic distributions to molecular docking. The chapter develops DiffDock-Glide, a hybrid approach in which diffusion-based pose generation is paired with physics-based refinement and scoring.
