\section{Biomolecular structure as a problem of distributions}
\label{ch1:sec:biomolecular-distributions}

Historically, structural biology focused on the native structure---the dominant, stable fold of a biomolecule. At the dawn of the field, this description was apt. The first structures of DNA (Watson and Crick, 1953), protein (myoglobin, Kendrew et al., 1958), and RNA (tRNA\textsuperscript{Phe}, Kim et al., 1974) were all well-determined: a single set of atomic coordinates, resolved to a precision that permitted detailed mechanistic interpretation. The native structure became both the goal of experimental determination and the target of computational prediction.

As the number of solved molecular structures grew, theorists sought first-principles descriptions of biomolecular folding. One particularly productive direction modeled the nearest-neighbor and pairwise interactions between polymeric units as an interacting set of spins. This theoretical treatment---the physics of spin glasses---had reached prominence in statistical mechanics for its capacity to predict ordered and disordered phases of a system from the pattern of interactions alone. Applied to proteins and nucleic acids, spin-glass theory predicted the existence of well-folded, metastable, and intrinsically disordered phases as consequences of heterogeneous , competeing interactions.

In the decades since, structural biology has migrated toward a viewpoint that treats ordered and disordered structures on equal footing. Experimental methods for resolving structure and dynamics have improved and expanded---NMR relaxation dispersion, single-molecule FRET, and cryo-electron microscopy now routinely reveal conformational heterogeneity that was invisible to earlier techniques. The theoretical discussion has shifted accordingly, centering on distinct structural states and their associated Boltzmann weights rather than on a single dominant fold.

The common object of interest to both the structural biologist and the spin-glass theorist is the equilibrium probability distribution---the function that assigns to each microstate a probability of being occupied at thermal equilibrium. For a molecule, a microstate is a configuration of atomic coordinates; for a spin lattice, it is an assignment of spin values to each site. In both cases, the equilibrium distribution is given by the Boltzmann law: the probability of a microstate decreases exponentially with its energy, at a rate set by the temperature. Low-energy configurations dominate at low temperatures; at high temperatures, entropic effects flatten the distribution and many configurations become accessible. The normalizing constant that converts these exponential weights into probabilities is the partition function, and its logarithm defines the free energy---the central quantity of equilibrium thermodynamics.

The Boltzmann distribution encodes everything one might wish to know about the thermodynamic behavior of a system: the probability of any conformation, the relative populations of competing states, and the response of those populations to changes in temperature or external conditions. But the elegant simplicity of this construction conceals its computational intractability. Computing the partition function requires evaluating the energy at every microstate and summing the results. For most systems of physical or biological interest, the configuration space is far too large. Levinthal's paradox illustrates the scale: a polypeptide of 100 residues, with only three conformational states per residue, would have roughly $10^{48}$ possible configurations. Exhaustive enumeration is not an option.

Spin-glass theory offers analytical tools for circumventing this enumeration---the replica trick, the cavity method---but these require simplifying assumptions about the structure of the interactions, typically that the couplings are drawn from a Gaussian distribution. Real biomolecular energy functions are more complex: they include bond stretches, angle bends, torsional potentials, electrostatics, van der Waals interactions, and solvent effects, none of which conform to the statistical regularities that make spin-glass calculations tractable. High-quality energy functions are necessarily complex in order to describe complex phenomena.

A final subtlety bridges the microscopic world of statistical mechanics to the macroscopic world of thermodynamics. The partition function is not a fixed quantity but depends on temperature, and its derivatives with respect to thermodynamic variables recover measurable quantities: the average energy, the heat capacity, the entropy. The equilibrium distribution is therefore not a single object but a family of distributions indexed by temperature and other environmental variables---ionic strength, ligand concentration, pressure. A complete description of a biomolecule's thermodynamics requires understanding how the distribution changes across this family, not merely characterizing it at a single condition.

The structure laid out so far is that the equilibrium distribution---and implicitly, the partition function---are of central interest in the biomolecular sciences, since they identify the accessible states of a system and their relative probabilities. However, direct computation faces three obstacles: the configuration space is overwhelmingly large, the analytical tools of statistical mechanics require idealizing assumptions that biomolecular systems violate, and the dependence on environmental variables introduces an additional axis of complexity. Fortunately, probabilistic generative models---a class of machine learning methods that has seen widespread adoption across scientific and engineering domains---provide a framework for parameterizing complex probability distributions from samples alone, without explicit access to the partition function. The following section introduces this class of models and describes the architectures most relevant to the molecular sciences.

\section{Generative models as a framework for molecular ensembles}
\label{ch1:sec:generative-models}

The previous section established that the equilibrium distribution is the central object of interest for biomolecular structure, but that computing it directly---by evaluating the partition function---is intractable for realistic systems. A different approach is to learn the distribution from samples. If one has access to configurations drawn from the equilibrium ensemble, whether from experiment or simulation, one can train a model to generate new samples with the same statistical properties. The partition function never needs to be computed; instead, it is implicitly encoded in the learned transformation from a simple reference distribution to the complex target.

This is the premise of probabilistic generative modeling. A probabilistic generative model learns to sample from a target distribution by constructing a mapping from a simple reference---typically a Gaussian---to the target. If the mapping is invertible and its Jacobian determinant is tractable, one can both generate new samples (by drawing from the reference and applying the forward map) and evaluate the likelihood of any configuration (by inverting the map and applying the change-of-variables formula). Different generative architectures differ in how they parameterize this transformation, but all share the property that the distribution is learned from data rather than specified analytically.

Normalizing flows were among the first probabilistic generative models to gain widespread attention in the machine learning literature. Early constructions, such as Real-NVP \citep{dinh2017} and related architectures, took the form of compositions of simple invertible layers, each designed to have a tractable Jacobian. The invertibility constraint is essential: it permits exact likelihood computation, which in turn enables training by maximum likelihood. A limitation of these discrete-layer flows is that the transformations they can represent are topologically constrained---a composition of continuous invertible maps cannot change the number of modes in a distribution, which poses challenges for the multimodal landscapes common in molecular systems.

The development of neural ordinary differential equations \citep{chen2018} opened a new direction. Continuous normalizing flows parameterize the transformation from reference to target as the solution of an ODE, $\frac{dx}{dt} = v_\theta(x, t)$, where $v_\theta$ is a neural network. The map is no longer a discrete composition of layers but a continuous flow in time, and the log-likelihood can be computed by integrating the divergence of the velocity field along the trajectory. This continuous-time perspective proved to be the direction in which probabilistic generative models were moving.

The connection between normalizing flows and molecular simulation was made explicit by \citet{noe2019}, who introduced Boltzmann generators---normalizing flows trained to sample from the Boltzmann distribution of molecular systems. The key insight was that a flow trained on molecular dynamics data could learn to map a Gaussian reference onto the equilibrium distribution, enabling one-shot generation of independent equilibrium samples without the sequential correlation of traditional simulation. Boltzmann generators demonstrated that generative models could produce thermodynamically meaningful samples, but they inherited the topological limitations of discrete flows: generating transitions between metastable states separated by high barriers remained difficult.

Diffusion models arose as an alternative construction that circumvents these topological constraints. Inspired by non-equilibrium thermodynamics \citep{sohl-dickstein2015}, diffusion models construct a stochastic process that progressively adds noise to data until the original structure is destroyed and the distribution converges to the reference. The forward process is fixed and requires no learning; what is learned is the reverse process---a neural network trained to predict, at each noise level, the direction toward higher-probability regions of the data distribution. Formally, this is equivalent to learning the score function, the gradient of the log-probability density with respect to the configuration \citep{song2019,ho2020}. Once the score is learned, the reverse process can be simulated to generate new samples: one draws from the Gaussian reference and iteratively denoises, following the learned score at each step, until a sample from the target distribution is obtained.

The score function has an intuitive geometric interpretation: at any point in configuration space, it points in the direction of steepest ascent toward higher probability. A diffusion model does not learn the probability density itself---which would require knowing the partition function---but rather this local gradient, which can be estimated from samples alone. This is why diffusion models sidestep the intractability of $Z$: they learn to navigate the probability landscape without ever needing to normalize it.

Because diffusion models operate through a stochastic process rather than a deterministic invertible map, they do not share the topological constraints of normalizing flows. The noise injection in the forward process erases the modal structure of the data, and the learned reverse process can reconstruct multiple disconnected modes from a single unimodal reference. This flexibility makes diffusion models particularly well suited to molecular systems, where the equilibrium distribution may consist of multiple metastable states separated by kinetic barriers.

Diffusion models have become a central pillar of probabilistic generative modeling, and several derivative methods have emerged that share the continuous-time, score-based perspective while varying the details of the forward process or the training objective. Conditional flow matching \citep{lipman2023} constructs a deterministic ODE that interpolates between reference and target, offering faster sampling than stochastic diffusion. Stochastic interpolants \citep{albergo2023} unify flow and diffusion perspectives within a common framework. These methods have found application across domains, from image synthesis to protein design.

Diffusion-based architectures have also become directly relevant to biomolecular structure prediction. AlphaFold 3 \citep{abramson2024} uses a diffusion module to generate atomic coordinates, representing a convergence between the generative modeling and structural biology communities. Yet as noted in the previous section, AlphaFold 3 and related methods struggle on RNA-only structures. The diffusion architecture is not the bottleneck; the limitation is that these models are trained predominantly on protein data and lack the physical grounding needed to generalize to RNA, where experimental structures are scarce and conformational heterogeneity is the rule rather than the exception.

This points to a fundamental tension in applying generative models to molecular science. The expressiveness of a neural network is a double-edged sword: the same flexibility that allows it to fit complex distributions also allows it to overfit sparse data or fail to generalize beyond the training distribution. Numerous studies have shown that generative models---insofar as they are neural networks---do not extrapolate reliably when trained on limited data \citep{zhang2021}. For molecular systems, where experimental structures are scarce and simulation data is expensive to generate, this limitation is acute. A generative model trained on a narrow slice of conformational space may interpolate well within that slice but fail entirely on out-of-distribution configurations. Overcoming this limitation requires building additional structure into the model---structure that reflects prior knowledge about the physical system the model is learning. This is the subject of the following section.

\section{The role of physical structure in generative modeling for molecular science}
\label{ch1:sec:physical-structure}
Inductive biases are constraints or structural assumptions built into a model that reduce its effective solution space and, by extension, the amount of data required to train it. In neural network design, inductive biases take many forms. One may build symmetry groups into the mathematical operations the network carries out---convolutional layers encode translation equivariance, and more recent architectures enforce SO(3) or SE(3) equivariance for three-dimensional molecular data \citep{thomas2018,fuchs2020}. Alternatively, one may encode physical knowledge into the loss function, as in physics-informed neural networks, which penalize violations of governing equations during training \citep{raissi2019}. The empirical pattern is consistent: models with appropriate inductive biases outperform unconstrained models when training data is limited, precisely because the bias excludes large regions of parameter space that are inconsistent with known structure.

Probabilistic generative models offer a distinctive opportunity to incorporate inductive biases: into the prior distribution and into the generative process that establishes a mapping between the target and the prior. A standard diffusion model maps the data distribution onto a fixed Gaussian prior, treating all training samples identically regardless of the conditions under which they were generated. But if the data carries additional structure---for instance, if different samples were collected at different temperatures---this structure can be encoded directly in the prior.

One of the primary contributions of this thesis is the Thermodynamic Maps framework (Chapter 4), which does exactly this. The construction begins with an observation: the reference distribution in a diffusion model is typically a standard Gaussian, but there is no requirement that it be so. One may instead choose a Gaussian whose variance depends on a physical parameter---temperature. The resulting reference distribution is equivalent to the equilibrium distribution of a harmonic oscillator, a system simple enough to be solved analytically yet rich enough to carry the correct temperature dependence. At low temperatures, the harmonic oscillator fluctuates narrowly around its minimum; at high temperatures, it explores a broader range of configurations. By conditioning the prior variance on the temperature at which each training sample was collected, the generative model learns not a single distribution but a family of distributions parameterized by temperature.

This construction enables a form of thermodynamic extrapolation that would be impossible for a model with a fixed prior. A Thermodynamic Map trained on samples from a small number of temperatures can generate samples at intermediate or extrapolated temperatures by simply adjusting the prior variance---the learned score function, which captures how the system deviates from harmonic behavior, transfers across the temperature range. Chapter 4 demonstrates this capability on the two-dimensional Ising model, where a Thermodynamic Map trained on data from just two temperatures---one above and one below the critical point---successfully predicts the temperature-dependent magnetization and heat capacity across the full range, including the phase transition itself. The inferred critical exponents agree with the exact Onsager solution. This is a striking result: the model has never seen data near the critical point, yet it infers the transition purely from the thermodynamic structure encoded in its architecture.

Concurrent and ongoing work extends this framework to other thermodynamic variables. Pressure, chemical potential, and composition can all be encoded in the prior distribution using analogous constructions, and more expressive reference distributions---mixtures, tilted Gaussians, or learned priors---may further improve the capacity of the model to represent complex thermodynamic landscapes. These extensions are discussed in the outlook.

Beyond encoding thermodynamics in the prior, a second strategy for incorporating physics into generative models is to combine learned and physics-based components in a hybrid workflow. Deep learning models excel at pattern recognition and can generate diverse candidate structures at low computational cost, but they may produce configurations that violate physical constraints---steric clashes, unrealistic bond geometries, or energetically unfavorable contacts. Physics-based models---molecular dynamics force fields, quantum mechanical calculations, empirical scoring functions---are grounded in physical law and can identify such violations, but they are computationally expensive and may be too slow to explore conformational space broadly. A natural strategy is to combine the two: use a generative model to propose candidates and a physics-based model to filter, refine, or score them.

This thesis pursues hybrid integration in two applications. In RNAnneal (Chapter 5), physics-based molecular dynamics simulations generate the conformational data on which a Thermodynamic Map ensemble is trained. The learned model then provides a thermodynamic score that ranks candidate RNA structures by their consistency with equilibrium---structures that resemble low-free-energy conformations score highly, while strained or unrealistic structures score poorly. Crucially, this scoring function is derived entirely from ab initio physical simulation; no experimental structures are used during training. The method is evaluated on riboswitches, where it recovers near-experimental conformations and provides interpretable measures of ensemble heterogeneity.

In DiffDock-Glide (Chapter 6), the hybrid strategy is applied to molecular docking---the prediction of how a small-molecule ligand binds to a protein target. Docking is not an ensemble prediction problem in the same sense as RNA folding, but it shares a common structure: one must search a large conformational space (ligand poses) and score candidates for physical plausibility. DiffDock \citep{corso2023} uses a diffusion model to generate candidate poses; DiffDock-Glide pairs this with Glide \citep{friesner2004}, a physics-based docking program, to refine and rescore the poses. The combination substantially improves performance on out-of-distribution targets---particularly AlphaFold-predicted structures, where the learned model alone produces steric clashes that the physics-based refinement corrects. The study demonstrates that the learned-plus-physics philosophy extends beyond thermodynamic ensemble prediction to other problems in computational molecular science.

The thesis is organized as follows. Chapter 2 establishes the methodological foundation by benchmarking three probabilistic generative frameworks---Neural Spline Flows, Conditional Flow Matching, and Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models---on molecular systems with tunable complexity. The results identify diffusion models as especially capable for the multimodal, moderate-dimensional distributions typical of molecular systems, motivating their use in subsequent chapters. Chapter 3 applies diffusion models to replica exchange molecular dynamics data, demonstrating that a model trained on multi-temperature simulations can generate equilibrium samples at temperatures absent from the training set. This chapter establishes that diffusion models can learn transferable thermodynamic information but does so through an ad hoc construction. Chapter 4 addresses this limitation by developing Thermodynamic Maps, the central methodological contribution of the thesis. The framework is validated on the Ising model and applied to RNA conformational sampling through Thermodynamic Map-accelerated Molecular Dynamics. Chapter 5 presents RNAnneal, which integrates Thermodynamic Maps into a complete pipeline for ab initio RNA ensemble prediction and represents the culmination of the methodological development. Chapter 6 extends the learned-plus-physics philosophy to molecular docking with DiffDock-Glide, demonstrating generality beyond RNA. A concluding chapter summarizes the contributions and discusses directions for future work.
